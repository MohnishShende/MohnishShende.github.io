Bro, the Sheep Is Doing Descartes

A field note in selective realism

Mohnish Shende · 2026

A phrase for the moment when someone is already accepting something completely absurd for the sake of a hypothetical, but suddenly decides that one particular detail is “unrealistic.”

It can also describe someone who takes a perfectly simple fact and constructs an enormous philosophical argument around it, usually to prove far more than the simple fact ever established.

The story

Imagine a leopard catches a sheep.

Not metaphorically.

The leopard has the sheep by the leg and is about to eat it.

The sheep, understandably, would prefer that this not happen. So the sheep screams:

Sheep: No, no, no, no. Wait. Hold on. Bro. Listen to me.

For reasons that will never be adequately explained, the leopard stops.

Sheep: Before you eat me, let me explain something.
Leopard: Fine. You have my attention. Explain why I shouldn't eat you.

The sheep realizes that its continued existence now apparently depends upon philosophy. So it begins.

Sheep: First, we have to establish something fundamental.
Leopard: Go on.
Sheep: I exist.
Leopard: Yes. I gathered that.
Sheep: No. You don't understand. I am.
Leopard: You are currently in my mouth, so I had reached a similar conclusion.
Sheep: But how do you know?

The leopard pauses. This has taken an unexpected turn.

The sheep is attempting something philosophers have been doing for a very long time: taking the most basic fact imaginable and asking whether it can actually be known with certainty.

One philosopher especially famous for doing this was René Descartes. Descartes tried to doubt everything he possibly could. Perhaps his senses were misleading him. Perhaps his memories were wrong. Perhaps everything he believed about the outside world was false.

But there appeared to be one thing he could not get rid of: the fact that the doubting itself was happening. That idea became famous in the expression:

Cogito, ergo sum.
I think, therefore I am.

The sheep has apparently read Descartes. This is already a problem.

Sheep: René Descartes argued that even if I doubt everything else, the fact that I am thinking means there must at least be something here doing the thinking—

The leopard slowly releases the sheep. This may require some time.

There is now, somehow, a table.

Nobody questions the table.

The leopard sits on one side. The sheep sits on the other. The leopard, having temporarily postponed dinner, has apparently agreed to conduct a seminar in philosophy with his intended lunch.

Leopard: Fine. Continue.
Sheep: I think, therefore I am.
Leopard: Fine. Suppose I grant that you exist.
Sheep: Excellent. Now we must establish whether my existence has intrinsic value.
Leopard: What does that mean?
Sheep: That my existence matters in itself.
Leopard: To whom?
Sheep: To me.
Leopard: Mine matters to me.
Sheep: Yes, but I am conscious.
Leopard: So am I.
Sheep: I experience suffering.
Leopard: I experience hunger.
Sheep: I have interests.
Leopard: I have lunch.

The sheep realizes that Descartes may not be sufficient. It begins searching for stronger arguments.

Perhaps being able to suffer gives the sheep a right not to be eaten. Perhaps conscious beings possess some special value. Perhaps there are moral rules that should apply even to hungry leopards. Perhaps existence itself gives the sheep certain rights.

The sheep is now attempting to construct an entire moral philosophy before dinner. The leopard is beginning to regret agreeing to this.

And then someone observing the thought experiment interrupts:

Observer: Wait a minute. This is ridiculous. A leopard wouldn't sit at a table and discuss philosophy with a sheep.

Everyone stops. There is silence.

Bro.
The sheep is doing Descartes.

The sheep can speak. The sheep understands abstract reasoning. The sheep knows a seventeenth-century French philosopher. The sheep understands an argument about whether existence can be known with certainty. The sheep is attempting to derive moral rights from consciousness while negotiating with a carnivore.

The leopard understands human language. The leopard understands philosophy. The leopard has voluntarily postponed eating. The leopard has agreed to hear a philosophical defense from his intended lunch.

There is a fucking table.

And now you're concerned that the leopard's behavior isn't zoologically realistic?

Bro.
The sheep is doing Descartes.

Realism left the premises twenty minutes ago.

What the phrase means

The phrase has two closely related meanings.

Meaning one: you accepted the absurdity already

A thought experiment asks you to temporarily accept something that may be impossible in reality so that you can examine what follows from it.

Suppose someone says: “Imagine a sheep that can speak and reason like a human.” You have two perfectly legitimate choices. You can say: “No. Sheep cannot speak. I reject the premise.” Fine. The thought experiment ends there.

Or you can say: “Fine. For the sake of the argument, this sheep can speak.” Now the hypothetical begins.

Once you have accepted the premise, however, you cannot casually switch standards halfway through because some later detail bothers you. You cannot spend ten minutes listening to the sheep discuss philosophy and then suddenly announce: “Actually, sheep cannot speak.”

Bro. We know. That was the premise. You accepted it. The sheep has been doing Descartes for ten minutes.

The same applies to the leopard. If you have already accepted a world containing a philosophically educated sheep capable of negotiating with its predator, complaining that the leopard is behaving unrealistically is a remarkably late moment to rediscover zoology.

You may reject the impossible premise. But reject it at the door. Do not enter the seminar, sit at the table, listen to the sheep discuss the nature of existence, and then complain that the leopard wouldn't really attend.

Meaning two: Professor Sheep

There is another joke hiding inside the first one. The sheep has a very simple problem: it does not want to die. That's it.

The leopard grabs it. The sheep struggles. No philosophy is required to explain this. The sheep does not need to prove that sheep are important to the universe. It does not need to demonstrate that Earth requires sheep. It does not need to establish that existence has objective value. It does not need to derive a universal moral law against eating sheep.

It wants to survive. So it tries to survive.

But Professor Sheep is not satisfied with that. Professor Sheep wants to turn “I want to continue existing” into “Therefore my existence has value.” Then: “Therefore my continued existence is morally justified.” Then perhaps: “Therefore my kind ought to continue existing.” And eventually: “Therefore reality is somehow better because my kind exists.”

Something important happened during that climb. The sheep began with a fact about itself: “I want to live.” But it ended with a claim about reality: “My existence ought to continue.” Those are not the same statement.

Wanting something does not automatically prove that the universe is obligated to provide it. The leopard eventually notices.

Leopard: Bro, are you explaining why you don't want to die, or are you trying to prove that the universe needs sheep?

That is Doing Descartes. Not because Descartes himself necessarily made that particular argument. His name has simply become part of the joke because Professor Sheep began his magnificent intellectual escape attempt with: “I think, therefore I am.”

Usage

Example I: Selective realism

Person A: Your hypothetical doesn't work. A leopard wouldn't understand the sheep's argument.
Person B: Bro, the sheep is doing Descartes.

Translation: We passed “realistic leopard behavior” several impossible assumptions ago.

Example II: Moving the reality goalposts

Person A: Fine. Imagine an advanced civilization of intelligent dolphins. But dolphins couldn't possibly establish universities underwater.
Person B: You accepted the advanced dolphin civilization three sentences ago. The sheep is doing Descartes.

Translation: If underwater universities are the part that bothers you, you may have entered the hypothetical with your skepticism calibrated incorrectly.

Example III: Professor Sheep appears

Person A: Humans understand their own existence. Therefore humanity must possess some special importance.
Person B: Careful. Professor Sheep is reaching for Descartes.

Translation: The first statement may be true. The conclusion still has to be earned.

Related terms

Professor Sheep
A person who begins with something relatively simple and constructs an enormous philosophical structure around it, eventually reaching conclusions much larger than the original observation established.

Person A: He's been explaining for forty minutes why the fact that humans can contemplate existence proves humanity has special significance in reality.
Person B: Professor Sheep.

Professor Sheep is not necessarily wrong. Professor Sheep simply has a lot of explaining left to do.
Doing Descartes
To introduce increasingly elaborate philosophical reasoning into a situation whose immediate reality either does not require it, or does not establish the conclusions being drawn from it.

The sheep doesn't merely want to escape the leopard anymore. He's doing Descartes.
The Leopard's Objection
The moment when an enormous argument is met with one extremely inconvenient question: “Fine. But what have you actually established?”

The Leopard's Objection does not require knowing philosophy. It requires listening carefully enough to notice when someone's conclusion has become larger than their evidence.
The Table
The absurd premise everyone has already accepted but conveniently forgotten about. The Table is invoked when someone begins demanding realism far too late in a thought experiment.

Person A: But that part isn't realistic.
Person B: There is already a table.

The existence of the Table is not to be investigated. The Table simply is.

The rule

A thought experiment is allowed to be impossible. That is often the point.

Talking sheep are allowed. Philosophical leopards are allowed. Cartesian livestock are allowed. Predators conducting seminars with their lunch are allowed. Tables appearing inexplicably in the wilderness are allowed.

You are always free to reject the premise. But once you accept it, you must allow the hypothetical enough room to do the job it was invented to do.

You may reject Professor Sheep. You may reject Professor Leopard. You may reject Descartes. You may reject the entire thought experiment. You may even reject the table.

But do it at the beginning.

Because once the leopard has released his lunch, sat down across from it, and listened patiently while the sheep explains why thinking proves existence, it is far too late to interrupt the proceedings with: “Actually, leopards don't do this.”

BRO.

The sheep is doing Descartes.
And there is already a fucking table.
